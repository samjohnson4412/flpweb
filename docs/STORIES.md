## Peter — evacuation lodging, Hurricane Milton

A college student forced to leave their dorm ahead of Milton. No family nearby
to call. Shelters wouldn't take their cat — their only companion. When Peter
contacted FLP, they said they planned to ride out the storm in their car.

Through the Hilton partnership, FLP placed Peter and their cat in a safe hotel.

> No fuss. No delay. Just help when it was needed most.

Peter later left a public testimonial:

> Wonderful, wonderful people. My cat and I have only been able to safely make it
> through Hurricane Milton because of the help given to us by this organization…
> I cannot convey enough how above and beyond they've gone in ensuring our safety.

**Video exists:** *The Road to Safety – Peter's Story*

---

## Maria — drone inspection → SBA loan, Hurricane Milton

Maria had bought her first home less than a year before Milton. The storm
severely damaged her roof.

FLP inspected the property, wrote a full report, and recommended repairs at no
cost. Critically, they told her she might qualify for an **SBA disaster loan** —
something she didn't know was available to a homeowner rather than a business.

She followed through. Months later she called to say the loan covered the damage
*and* funded mitigation against future storms: no interest for 12 months, a year
before payments began, under 4% interest.

> That's the power of knowledge, and standing beside someone when the paperwork
> and red tape feel heavier than the damage itself.

**Video exists:** *Roofs, Relief & Resilience*

## Amy — back-to-back storms, Helene *and* Milton

A single mother with two kids, two cats, and a dog. Steady job, reliable car,
stable home, a solid plan.

**Hurricane Helene** flooded her house and totaled her vehicle. Days later —
before an insurance adjuster could even visit — **Hurricane Milton** arrived.
No car, no backup plan, nowhere to go.

FLP arranged safe transportation and lodging for Amy and her entire household,
pets included, more than 100 miles away, hours before landfall — through the
Hilton partnership and what the script calls "a very complex Uber ride."

> She wasn't struggling until she suddenly was. And she couldn't just order an
> Uber to the nearest public shelter and expect six living beings to be admitted.
>
> There were no forms, no waitlists, no delays. Just help. Fast, human, and real.

## Real questions the hotline answered

Quoted verbatim in the script. These are more persuasive than any description of
the service could be:

> "Can I still leave if I'm in Zone B?"
>
> "My window is cracked. What do I do?"
>
> "My daughter's on a ventilator, and I'm staying. How can I keep the power on?"

The script's own framing of why this matters:

> Official hotlines are helpful, but their advice can be too generic — like being
> told to "seek local public shelter" when there's no shelter within 30 miles and
> no car to get you there.

## The pattern

All three stories share a shape: **the official system had a gap, and FLP filled
it.** Shelters that won't take pets. An SBA program nobody told her about. An
insurance adjuster who couldn't arrive before the next storm did.

That's the organization's actual position — not "we help families," which every
charity says, but "we are what happens when the system's answer doesn't fit your
situation." The homepage should say something close to that.

Note that **pets appear in every single story**: Peter's cat, Amy's two cats and
a dog, Nicole Shanklin's three cats. Shelters refusing animals is a documented
reason families don't evacuate. This is a real, specific, ownable position.
