## History: the light show (Sylvan Ramble Lights)

- Started in 2015, when Sam was 16, in a front yard on Sylvan Ramble Street in South Tampa.
- Halloween and Christmas shows choreographed to music, with flame, water and smoke effects. Ran 2015 to 2023, skipping 2022: eight seasons.
- More than 20,000 visitors over its lifetime, more than 10,000 in the final year, and 500+ on peak nights.
- Raised about $50,000. Most of it went to other charities, including more than $9,000 to Clothes To Kids (180+ weekly wardrobes) and $9,014.45 to Where Love Grows for fighting child hunger. In the last two years, 9% was retained, so say "nearly every dollar", never "every dollar".
- Press: NBC Nightly News, Tampa Bay Times (2021, 2023), Fox 13 (two pieces), ABC Action News, WFLA, Bay News 9 (2021, 2023), Taste & See Tampa Bay, BB's Adventures, and Tampa Prep.
- A haunted house once drew about 300 people on one Halloween night and raised little.
- Public reason the show ended: it outgrew a residential street. Don't make it about the neighbor; this rule is relaxed, but still the default.

## 2024: Hurricanes Helene and Milton

- Helene made landfall September 26, 2024. Milton formed nine days later and reached Florida on October 9, 13 days after Helene. Use "13 days apart"; avoid "nine days" and "two weeks".
- FLP assisted 50+ individuals and families, with no staff and a $0 operating budget, working entirely through partnerships.
- 20+ people were placed in emergency lodging in the days before Milton.
- On October 6, FLP secured discounted Hilton rooms in Orlando at $68 a night; the post was shared 700 times. That contact is gone, and there is no current hotel partner.
- From October 8, FLP offered free one-month eSIM talk-and-text plans.
- From October 11, FLP did free drone roof and home documentation across Tampa Bay, dozens of properties in all.
- Ran a storm line and plain-language briefings: tornado warnings, wind-gust forecasts, evacuation questions.
- **Stories, with permission and first names only:**
  - **Peter:** a college student ordered out of a dorm with a cat no shelter would take, placed in a hotel. Doing much better two years on, and tells it in the film "The Road to Safety". The site uses they/them for Peter.
  - **Amy:** a single mother with two kids, two cats and a dog. Helene flooded her house and totaled her car, and FLP moved all six of them 100+ miles inland before Milton.
  - **Maria:** a first-time homeowner whose FLP report led to an SBA loan under 4% that covered repairs and mitigation.

## What FLP offers now

- **Storm line:** a person answers (813) 733-8806, by phone, about evacuation zones, windows, medical equipment, pets and similar questions.
- **Evacuation and transportation help**, and help finding lodging that takes the whole family, pets included. FLP helps find it; it doesn't promise to pay.
- **Phone and data plans** (eSIM) for people who lose service.
- **Drone roof and property documentation** with a written report, flown under FAA Part 107. Free year-round for low-income households. Call it "documentation" or "a survey", not a home inspection, until the license is issued.
- **After a storm:** wellness checks, emergency repairs, debris removal and cleanup, family stability support (including toys for children), and help through FEMA, SBA and insurance paperwork.
- **SBA disaster loans:** say they are open to homeowners and renters, not only businesses.
- **For organizations** (churches, pantries, schools, clinics) through the Partner form: documentation, aid navigation, connectivity (phone and data plans, charging, internet access) and a storm line for the people they serve.
- **Volunteer roles:** storm hotline, supply distribution, inspections support, cleanup crews, events, and ambassadors.
- **Supplies:** in-kind items and supply drives. There is no current wishlist.
- **Sponsorship:** goods, services, and event sponsorship (Beacons of Hope, and Lanterns of Hope if it happens). No program or storm sponsorship.
- **Lantern Keeper** monthly giving. The tiers illustrate cost and are not restricted:
  - $10: keeps the storm line answered
  - $25: about one drone documentation a year
  - $50: several nights of lodging a year
  - $100: phone and data plans

### The cheapest help is the help that arrives early

Most of what makes a hurricane survivable is decided weeks before landfall — whether you have a plan, whether you have documentation, whether anyone nearby knows you’re there.

*Section: Services*

Free

#### Make a plan with us

Call the storm line before the season, or as soon as a storm has a name. We’ll help you work out when to leave, where to go, and who to tell.

Free

#### A plan for your pets

Many shelters won’t take animals, and that is one of the most common reasons families don’t leave. We help you find somewhere that takes everyone, before you need it.

Free

#### Know your aid options

SBA disaster loans, FEMA assistance and local programs exist for homeowners and renters. We’ll tell you what you may be able to apply for before you need it.

#### Document your home now

Walk through your home with your phone before a storm: every room, every side of the house, the roof from the ground, and your receipts. Save it somewhere off your phone.

*Section: Why pre-storm documentation matters*

Documentation

#### Why pre-storm documentation matters

After Milton, the families who struggled most weren’t always the ones with the worst damage. They were the ones who couldn’t prove what the damage replaced.

Insurers ask for before-and-after. Most people only have after. Ten minutes with your phone before a storm is the cheapest protection a claim can have.

Call the storm line at (813) 733-8806 or send us a message. A person will help you work out what to do before the next storm.

#### Maria

Hurricane Milton · inspection and aid navigation

Maria had bought her first home less than a year before Milton. The storm badly damaged her roof.

We inspected the property, wrote a full report, and recommended repairs — at no cost. More importantly, we told her she might qualify for an SBA disaster loan, something she had no idea was available to a homeowner rather than a business.

She followed through. Months later she called to tell us the loan covered the damage and funded work to prevent the same thing happening again: no interest for twelve months, a year before payments began, at under 4%.

The inspection was free. What it unlocked was worth considerably more than the inspection.

Helene came up Florida’s Gulf coast in late September 2024. At the time we were still Sylvan Ramble Lights — a holiday light show charity that raised money for other people’s causes and had never run a relief operation.

On 27 September we posted this:

**URGENT.** We are stepping outside of our normal mission to provide urgent support for those impacted by Hurricane Helene. If you are in need of assistance or would like to donate to those in need, visit our website.

100% of the funds raised will go directly to those in need, ensuring they receive immediate help during this challenging time.

Facebook, 27 September 2024

We had no disaster budget, no protocols, and no staff. What we had was a community that trusted us, a list of businesses who had said yes before, and a willingness to make phone calls.

Our response

#### What we did

What the surge left on the road. Boats move; so does everything else.

#### Direct financial assistance

100% pass-through, no administrative deduction.

#### Emergency shelter

Through hotel partnerships negotiated in the days before landfall.

#### Transportation

#### Cleanup support

Volunteer crews in the days afterwards.

*Section: Then it happened again*

Thirteen days later

#### Then it happened again

Milton formed nine days after Helene’s landfall and reached Florida on 9 October, 13 days after it.

Families we had just helped were hit a second time before insurance adjusters could reach the first round of damage. One of them was Amy — a single mother with two children, two cats and a dog, whose house Helene had flooded and whose car it had totaled.

*\[Button: Hurricane Milton → /response/milton-2024\]*

*Section: What we learned*

Lessons

#### What we learned

#### Speed beats process.

Every hour spent designing an intake procedure was an hour a family spent not knowing if help was coming. We got faster by deciding sooner, not by planning better.

#### Existing relationships are the whole asset.

We had no money. What we had was eight years of local businesses knowing who we were, and that turned out to be convertible into hotel rooms.

#### Storms don’t queue.

Planning for one event at a time is planning wrong.

#### What we can help with

#### Documented damage inspections

Once winds drop and it is safe to fly, an FAA-certified drone pilot will survey your roof and property and give you a written report. Free. You keep it and use it however you choose.

#### Help finding aid

SBA disaster loans are open to homeowners, not only businesses, and almost nobody is told that. We will walk through FEMA, SBA and local programs with you and help you file.

#### Phone and data plans

Phone or data plans for anyone who loses service. If you cannot reach your family, you cannot do anything else.

#### A line a person answers

Not a recording. Ask about your evacuation zone, your windows, medical equipment, or what to do with your animals.

Where we are

#### Being honest about where we are

We are based in Tampa. This storm is forecast for the Panhandle, six hours up the road, in counties where almost nobody has heard of us. Writing that on our own website is better than pretending otherwise.

So the most useful thing we can do this week is not to arrive unannounced. It is to be reachable, to coordinate through county emergency management and 211 rather than around them, and to come back in the weeks afterward — when the news trucks have gone and people are still trying to get a roof documented and a claim moving. *Section: What a Lantern Keeper is*

Monthly giving

#### What a Lantern Keeper is

A Lantern Keeper gives a set amount every month. That’s the whole idea. It is unglamorous and it is the single most useful thing a supporter can do for a disaster relief organization.

Nearly everything else we raise is episodic — a gala, a campaign, a storm that makes the news. But the calls don’t arrive on that schedule, and the expensive moment is always the one before landfall, when a room has to be booked tonight and reimbursement is a problem for later.

Monthly gifts are the reserve that lets us commit first and account afterwards.

*Section: What it pays for*

Where it goes

#### What it pays for

Readiness

#### \$10 a month

Keeps the storm line answered through a season.

Documentation

#### \$25 a month

Roughly a post-storm drone inspection every year — the report a family hands to an insurer, or that unlocks an SBA loan.

Shelter

#### \$50 a month

Several nights of emergency lodging a year for a family with nowhere to go and animals no shelter will take.

Connectivity

#### \$100 a month

Phone and data plans for families who lose service after a storm.

These are honest illustrations of what things cost us, not restricted designations. Gifts go to wherever the need is sharpest that month.

*Section: Why monthly beats an annual gift of the same size*

The case for monthly

#### Why monthly beats an annual gift of the same size

- **It arrives before the storm.** A December gift doesn’t help a family evacuating in October.
- **It lets us commit fast.** Knowing what next month looks like is the difference between “we’ll see what we can do” and “we’ve booked it.”
- **It costs almost nothing to steward.** No venue, no auction, no printing. Close to the entire gift reaches the work.
- **It makes us fundable.** Grantmakers look for a stable base before they’ll commit. Recurring donors are that base.

## Page: /about (floridalanternproject.org/about)

*Section: Hero*

About us

### We started as a light show

That sounds like a non sequitur until you notice that both jobs are the same one: get light to where it’s dark, and get people to show up for each other.

*Section: Our story*

2015 to now

#### Our story

In 2015 a computerized holiday light show went up in a front yard on Sylvan Ramble Street in South Tampa. It grew — flame effects, water, smoke, thousands of individually controllable lights choreographed to music — and it grew again. On peak nights more than 500 people came. Over the years more than 20,000 did, and the show raised roughly \$50,000 for local charities feeding and clothing Tampa Bay children. It ended up on NBC Nightly News.

By 2023 it had outgrown a residential street. We looked at larger venues. Then, in 2024, Hurricanes Helene and Milton arrived 13 days apart, and the question of where to put a light show stopped being the interesting one.

We stepped outside our mission to help, with no budget and no plan beyond the phone numbers of people who owed us favors. We booked hotel rooms. We drove people inland. We flew a drone over damaged roofs and wrote up what we saw so families could file claims. By the end of the year we had assisted more than 50 individuals and families.

In 2025 we became the Florida Lantern Project. The name changed; the idea didn’t. We still bring light. We just try to do it before the dark part.
